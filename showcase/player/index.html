The Last Nonnas: Inside Italy's Race to Save Its Grandmothers' Recipes

C.B.
Chiara Bellini · Italy Correspondent · August 4, 2026 · 8 min read
Orecchiette on a tavoliere, the wooden board traditionally used to shape them by hand. Photo: Giuseppe Nigro / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

In a farmhouse kitchen outside Bari, Maria Lorusso is doing what she has done most mornings for the better part of seven decades: dragging a small knife across a rope of semolina dough, flicking each piece into the little ear-shaped curl that gives orecchiette its name. She doesn't measure the flour. She doesn't check a clock. Her hands simply know when the dough is ready, the way they've known since she was seven years old, standing on a stool next to her own grandmother.

Maria is 84. She has, by her own estimate, taught fewer than a dozen people to make pasta this way — and none of them, she says with a shrug that is somehow both resigned and amused, are under the age of forty. Across southern Italy, this scene is becoming rarer by the year. The nonnas who hold the region's culinary memory are aging out of their kitchens, and much of what they know was never written down in the first place.

A Cuisine Passed Down, Not Written Down

Italian home cooking has always been an oral tradition. Regional dishes vary not just from province to province but from kitchen to kitchen, shaped by whatever a family happened to grow, raise, or trade with a neighbor. A ragù in Bologna bears only a passing resemblance to one from Naples; a loaf of bread in Puglia is a different animal entirely from one baked two hours north. None of it was standardized, because none of it needed to be — it was simply what your nonna made, and you learned it by watching, not by reading.

That informality is precisely what makes it fragile now. As younger generations move to cities for work, and as the number of people willing to spend four hours making Sunday lunch shrinks with each decade, entire regional repertoires risk disappearing with the women who hold them. Food historians estimate that dozens of hyper-local pasta shapes, sauces, and preserving techniques have already been lost in the last fifty years, surviving only in the memories of people now in their eighties and nineties.

"Nobody wrote a cookbook for us. We were the cookbook."

The Race to Record What's Left

A small but growing movement is trying to close that gap before it closes for good. Community cooking schools across Puglia, Sicily, and Emilia-Romagna have begun formal apprenticeship programs pairing nonnas with paid students — often young chefs or food writers — specifically to document techniques that have never appeared in any recipe book. Sessions are filmed, measurements are estimated after the fact by weighing what the nonna produced by feel, and the resulting notes are published to small regional archives rather than any single national database.

It is slow, unglamorous work. A single dish can take a full day to properly document, and the nonnas themselves are not always easy interview subjects — many insist there is nothing to explain, that the dough simply tells you what it needs. Getting that instinct into words that a stranger could follow is, more than one archivist has admitted, harder than it sounds.

What Gets Lost When a Kitchen Goes Quiet

The stakes go beyond nostalgia. Researchers studying Italy's regional food traditions argue that hyper-local cooking knowledge is also a record of agricultural history — which grains grew where, which vegetables were preserved for winter, which cuts of meat a poorer household made stretch across a week. Lose the recipe, and you often lose the only remaining account of how people actually lived.

For Maria, the motivation is simpler. She's not thinking about food history when she rolls out dough at six in the morning. She's thinking about her granddaughter, who visits on Sundays now, flour on her hands for the first time, learning the same curl of the wrist that was taught to Maria by a woman who never once wrote it down.
